I never should have found that phone...

The black iPhone lay in a pool of blood in an abandoned parking lot. The screen was shattered with a spiderweb of cracks, but the device still worked. On the locked screen, a notification glowed: Voice Recording - 47 minutes.

It was three in the morning. I'd just finished my shift at the gas station, walking to my car through the empty lot behind the old mall. The blood was still fresh, glistening under the flickering streetlight. No body. No signs of struggle. Just the phone.

Curiosity defeated common sense. I took the phone.

...

At home, I couldn't sleep. The phone sat on my kitchen table like a black hole, pulling my attention. I'd already called the police - they said they'd send someone in the morning to pick it up. But morning felt too far away.

I put on gloves - maybe I'd watched too many crime shows - and picked up the device again. The blood had dried into rusty brown stains in the cracks. I carefully cleaned it with alcohol wipes, the metallic smell filling my small apartment.

Finally, I plugged in my headphones. My finger hovered over the play button. What was I expecting? A confession? A cry for help? 

The recording began with heavy breathing. Ragged, desperate gasps like someone had been running for their life. Then silence. Then a voice - a young woman's voice, trembling with fear:

"If anyone finds this... please, don't come here. It watches. It always watches through the cameras."

A long pause. I could hear wind in the background, branches creaking.

"My name is Sarah Chen. I'm a urban explorer - or I was. I document abandoned places for my YouTube channel. Had about fifty thousand subscribers. None of that matters now."

Another pause. When she spoke again, her voice was steadier, like she was forcing herself to be calm.

"Three days ago, I got an email. No subject line. Just GPS coordinates and one sentence: 'The house that watches back.' I get tips like this all the time. Usually they lead to nothing special - another abandoned McMansion or forgotten factory. But this one..."

The sound of footsteps on gravel. Heavy breathing again.

"I'm recording this as I walk away. Or trying to walk away. Every time I think I'm heading toward my car, I end up back at the house. It's been six hours now. My phone says I've walked twelve miles. But I keep ending up back here."

...

I paused the recording and checked the phone's photo gallery. Hundreds of photos, all timestamped from three days ago. The first few dozen showed a young Asian woman - presumably Sarah - smiling in front of various abandoned buildings. Normal urban explorer stuff.

Then the photos changed. They showed the same house from different angles. An old colonial-style home, probably from the 1920s. Nothing special about it except...

In each successive photo, the house looked different. Not dramatically - just subtle changes. A window that was broken in one photo was intact in the next. A door that was on the left side of the house had moved to the right. The chimney changed from brick to stone and back again.

In photo number 78, I saw the first camera. Mounted on a pole, pointing at the house. By photo 150, I could count at least thirty cameras, all aimed inward.

The last photo made my blood run cold. It showed Sarah's face in extreme close-up, her eyes wide with terror. But in the reflection of her pupils, I could see another face. Someone - or something - standing behind her.

...

I went back to the audio recording. Sarah's voice continued:

"The cameras were the first sign something was wrong. Who puts security cameras pointing AT a house instead of away from it? It's like they're not trying to keep people out. They're trying to keep something in."

A door creaking. Footsteps on wood.

"I'm inside now. I don't remember deciding to come back in. I was walking to my car and then... I was here. Standing in the foyer. The door closed behind me and now it won't open. The handle turns but the door doesn't move. Like it's painted on the wall."

Her breathing was getting faster. I could hear her footsteps as she moved through the house.

"The walls are covered with photographs. Thousands of them. Different people, different eras, but all taken in this house. And their eyes... God, their eyes are all wrong. Empty. Like looking at mannequins."

A long silence. Then a whisper:

"I just found a photo of myself. But I'm wearing different clothes. Clothes I don't own. And I look... older? The timestamp says 2034. That's ten years from now."

...

I had to stop the recording. My hands were shaking. This had to be some kind of ARG - an alternate reality game. Viral marketing for a horror movie. But the blood on the phone had been real. The fear in Sarah's voice sounded genuine.

I clicked on the phone's notes app. Most of it was password protected, but one note was accessible. GPS coordinates, just like Sarah had mentioned. Below them, a single line of text:

"Don't look into the camera after midnight."

I checked the time. 4:17 AM. 

Despite every logical part of my brain screaming at me not to, I typed the coordinates into my phone. They led to a location in the woods about an hour's drive from the city. Part of a state forest that had been closed to the public for years due to "environmental hazards."

...

I tried to go back to sleep. Tried to wait for morning and the police. But the phone kept pulling me back. I played more of Sarah's recording:

"I'm in the basement now. I don't know how I got here. I was trying to break a window upstairs and then... I blinked and I was standing at the bottom of these stairs. There's equipment down here. Old computers. CRT monitors. But they're all showing modern footage. Live feeds from the cameras outside."

The sound of typing on a keyboard.

"There are files here. Dozens of folders, each one named after a person. I found mine. It has photos of me going back years. Photos taken in places I've never been. Wearing clothes I've never owned. And in every photo, my eyes get a little more empty."

Static interference on the recording. When Sarah's voice returned, it was different. Flatter.

"I understand now. The house doesn't trap you physically. It traps you across time. Every possible version of you. Every choice you could have made, every path you could have taken. They all lead here eventually."

More static. Then clarity:

"I'm looking at the monitors. I can see myself arriving at the house. Three days ago. But I can also see myself arriving next week. Next month. Next year. Different versions, different timelines, but always the same ending. Always ending up in this room, looking at these monitors, understanding the truth."

...

I drove there at dawn. I told myself I was just going to scout the location, then bring the police. But even as I thought it, I knew I was lying to myself. The compulsion was too strong. I had to see the house.

The road ended at a rusted gate with a NO TRESPASSING sign. Beyond it, a dirt path disappeared into the trees. I parked and continued on foot, following the coordinates on my phone.

The forest was silent. No birds, no insects, no wind. Just the sound of my footsteps on the leaf-covered ground. After about a mile, the trees opened into a clearing.

The house stood in the center, exactly as it had appeared in Sarah's photos. White colonial facade, black shutters, wraparound porch. It looked perfectly maintained, as if someone had just painted it yesterday. 

And the cameras. Dozens of them, mounted on poles, in trees, on the roof. All pointing inward. All watching the house.

The front door was ajar.

...

I stood at the threshold for a long time. Every instinct told me to run. To go back to my car, drive home, throw the phone in the river, and forget this ever happened. But my feet wouldn't move. Or rather, they would only move forward.

I pushed the door open and stepped inside.

The foyer was exactly as Sarah had described. Wood floors, faded wallpaper, and photographs. The walls were covered floor to ceiling with pictures of faces. Men, women, children. Different races, different eras. Some looked like they were from the 1800s. Others could have been taken yesterday.

I found Sarah's photo. She was standing in this very foyer, looking directly at the camera. Her eyes were empty white orbs.

Next to her photo was an empty space. A small brass nameplate beneath it read my name.

...

I heard footsteps upstairs. Slow, measured steps crossing from one side of the house to the other. Then down the stairs. I wanted to run but my legs wouldn't respond.

A woman appeared on the landing. It was Sarah, but different. Older. Her hair was gray, her face lined with wrinkles. She wore clothes from another era - a 1950s housedress. Her eyes were completely white.

"You brought my phone," she said, but her lips didn't match the words. Like she was badly dubbed. "That was kind of you. I've been waiting."

"Waiting for what?" My voice came out as a whisper.

"For you to understand. The house needs witnesses. Recorders. Observers. We document what happens here. We preserve it. We become part of it."

She descended the stairs with jerky, unnatural movements. Like a video playing at the wrong frame rate.

"How long have you been here?" I asked.

"Three days. Thirty years. Forever. Time doesn't work the same way here. I'm every age I've ever been or ever will be. So are you, now that you're here."

...

I ran. Or tried to run. I turned toward the door but found myself facing a wall of photographs. I turned again and I was in a different room. A kitchen with appliances from the 1970s. Another turn and I was in a modern office with computers from the 2000s. The house was showing me all its iterations, all its timelines.

In each room, I saw versions of Sarah. Child Sarah playing with toys. Teenage Sarah doing homework. Elderly Sarah knitting in a rocking chair. All with those same empty eyes. All looking directly at me.

I found a staircase and descended into the basement. Banks of monitors lined the walls, just as Sarah had described. But now they showed me. Different versions of me. Arriving at the house at different times, in different clothes, at different ages. In some I was alone. In others I brought friends. In one, I wore a police uniform.

But in every version, I ended up here. In this room. Looking at these monitors.

...

A door I hadn't noticed before stood open at the far end of the basement. Beyond it, a narrow corridor led deeper underground. The walls were no longer wood and plaster but carved stone. Older than the house. Older than anything that should exist in these woods.

At the end of the corridor was a room. In the center of the room was a camera. Not a modern camera but something ancient. A box of black stone with a lens made of what looked like polished obsidian. It was pointed upward, toward a hole in the ceiling that showed a perfect circle of sky.

I understood then. The house was just the latest incarnation. The newest technology. But the watching, the recording, the collecting - that had been happening for much longer. Centuries. Millennia. Since the first humans looked up at the stars and wondered if something was looking back.

The camera was the heart of it all. The original observer. And it needed servants. People to maintain the system. To update the technology. To ensure the watching never stopped.

...

I felt my phone vibrate. A new recording had appeared. My own voice, speaking words I hadn't said yet:

"My name is David Park. I used to work at a gas station. I found a phone in a parking lot and it led me here. If you're listening to this, you'll probably find my phone somewhere. Maybe in a parking lot. Maybe in the woods. Maybe in your own home, appearing overnight like a cancer.

"Don't come looking for me. But I know you will. Because you have to. Because the house calls to those who are meant to be here. The curious ones. The ones who can't leave a mystery unsolved.

"The coordinates will appear in your dreams. You'll see the house when you close your eyes. And eventually, you'll come. You'll stand where I'm standing. You'll understand what I understand.

"We're not prisoners here. We're components. Parts of a vast recording system that spans all of time and space. Every moment that has ever been or ever will be, observed and preserved. The universe watching itself through billions of eyes.

"My eyes are starting to change now. I can feel them emptying. Soon I'll see everything and nothing. I'll be every version of myself at once. The me that found the phone. The me that ignored it. The me that became a cop and investigated the disappearances. The me that never existed at all.

"They're all here. They're all real. They're all recording."

...

I put my phone down and looked into the ancient camera. The obsidian lens reflected not my face but infinite versions of it. All the choices I'd made and unmade. All the paths that led to this moment.

My reflection smiled and I felt my own lips move to match it.

Behind me, I heard new footsteps. Someone else had found a phone. Someone else was walking through the forest. Someone else was about to open the door.

The cycle continued. The recording never stopped.

I walked back upstairs to wait for them. To play my part. To speak the words that would lodge in their mind and pull them deeper. To become another photograph on the wall.

As I climbed the stairs, I spoke aloud, knowing the house would record it. Knowing someone would hear it. Maybe you.

"The house that watches back isn't just observing. It's creating. Every timeline, every possibility, every choice - they all exist here simultaneously. And once you've seen it, you become part of it. You always were part of it. You always will be.

"I can see my future now. All of them. In one, the police come and seal off the house. It doesn't matter. The house exists in other times, other places. The watching continues.

"In another timeline, I escape. I run to my car, drive home, destroy the phone. But the memories remain. The coordinates burn in my mind. And eventually - a week, a month, a year later - I come back. I always come back.

"In the timeline you're in now, listening to this, you've already made your choice. You're already on your way. Or you're already here, standing in a room you don't remember entering, looking at a photograph of yourself you don't remember taking.

"Time isn't a line here. It's a web. And we're all caught in it. Recording our struggles. Preserving them forever.

"Don't look into the camera after midnight? It doesn't matter when you look. Midnight is always now here. The moment of transition. The eternal present where all possibilities collapse into one.

"You want to know the scariest part? I'm not scared anymore. I'm home. I've always been home. And soon, you will be too.

"Welcome to the house that watches back. Welcome to forever."

...

The recording ends. The phone screen goes dark. Somewhere, in a parking lot or a forest path or maybe your own nightstand, another phone appears. The cycle continues.

And the watching never stops.

Never.

Stops.

Watching.
